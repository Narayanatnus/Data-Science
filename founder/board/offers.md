# Offers lens
Score: 6 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **Market (3 of 4 strong).** The pain is massive and recurring: working parents cannot work without care, and it is a monthly need, not a one-off. Buyers in a suburb like Naperville are likely to have money (verify local household income and the share of dual-earner families with /founder-consumer). They are easy to find: parents of 1-to-6-year-olds cluster around schools, employers and parent networks. Whether demand is growing locally is unknown (/founder-consumer).
- **Value equation.** Dream outcome is high (a safe child who is learning, and parents free to work). Time to result is near-instant: care starts on the first day of enrolment. Effort is low: drop-off and pick-up. Likelihood gets a head start from a national brand and a set curriculum, which gives a new center proof it could not have on its own.
- **Retention.** Once a child settles, switching is painful for the family, so a filled seat tends to stay filled for years. A stack built for retention pays back.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The money does not cover the box.** Franchise fee, royalties, marketing fund and build-out are all unknown. Licensed child care centers often cost more to open than $650,000, especially new builds, and this needs verifying against the franchise disclosure document. Labour is set by staff-to-child ratios, which are fixed by state licensing, so margin depends mainly on how full the center is and the age mix (toddler rooms need more staff per child than preschool rooms). If the cash runs out before enrolment ramps, the offer never gets a chance (/founder-cfo).
2. **Commodity in a crowded market.** At $1,300-$1,400 a month, if parents see it as "a center like the others nearby", it competes on price and availability. Prices and waitlists of nearby centers are unknown (/founder-competitors). A franchise curriculum other chains also offer is not a difference parents feel.
3. **Likelihood is only as good as the staff.** For parents, proof is about the people, not the brand: teacher turnover, safety record, and how the child is doing. Hiring and keeping qualified staff is a known problem in this industry; one bad incident or a revolving door of teachers destroys perceived likelihood. The operator role is not yet defined.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Who exactly is the buyer: which age group, which neighbourhood, which employers? How many families within a short drive, and how many seats are already open nearby?
- What is the full opening cost from the disclosure document, and how many enrolled children does it take to break even?
- Why would a parent pick you over the center they drive past? What can you add (longer hours, daily photo and progress updates, meals included, help with the move to kindergarten) that is cheap to deliver and that parents value?
- What is the guarantee? For example, a first month that can be refunded if the child does not settle, or a fixed, written staff-to-child ratio that beats the legal minimum.
- Is the scarcity real: a founding-family rate or a limited number of pre-opening seats, with the waitlist to prove it?
- Could you charge more than the local average and still be the obvious choice? If not, why not?
- Who runs the center day to day, and what is their child care track record?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. A full cost model from the franchise disclosure document (fees, royalties, build-out, working capital) that fits inside $650,000 with a cash buffer and shows break-even enrolment (/founder-cfo).
2. A competitor map showing local prices, capacity and waitlists, with a clear gap this center can own (/founder-competitors), plus evidence of demand at the planned tuition (/founder-consumer).
3. A written offer stack that goes beyond the brand: named extras, a guarantee the numbers can afford, and real pre-opening scarcity (/founder-offer).
4. A named, qualified center director and a staffing plan that meets licensing ratios for the planned age mix.
